Juan Pablo Durango Cardona

I am a Colombian biologist, with a deep interest in the amphibians and reptiles of the Neotropics. My research focuses on bioacoustics and taxonomy — using frog calls to understand species boundaries and evolutionary relationships in one of the most diverse regions on earth. I have done fieldwork across Colombia, especially in the Chocoan rainforest and the Andean cloud forests, and have collaborated on environmental assessments, conservation planning, and biological collections work. I am currently based in Medellín and open to collaborations, field projects, and graduate opportunities.

About me

My story

I am a biologist from Colombia, born into a family with deep rural roots. My father and my grandparents were displaced by violence around the time of my birth, forcing my family to seek safety in Medellín. Life in the city was difficult, and we ended up moving back and forward between the city and the countryside. There, I attended a rural school and walked for more than four hours each day through patches of jungle, rivers, and pastures. Along the way, I discovered the natural world with fascination — watching tadpoles in the on the side of the road, chasing birds and snakes, or sometimes running in terror from fierce cows. Those walks nurtured my curiosity for nature.

Later, we returned to the city and I finished high school. Thanks to my strong results on the national exams, I earned a government scholarship and became the first in my family to attend university. While studying biology, I returned to the same village of my childhood to conduct fieldwork. Walking those familiar paths with colleagues, I encountered a poison dart frog that I had heard—and that is part of the soundscape of my childhood—but had never seen: Andinobates opisthomelas. Its bright red color captivated me instantly—so much so that I ended up doing my thesis on it.

To support myself during university, I worked alongside my studies—always seeking opportunities related to biology. I participated in consulting projects, conducted environmental fieldwork and wrote impact assessments, which sharpened my analytical skills. I also had the privilege of working in the science education and outreach section at the Museo de Ciencias Naturales de La Salle, where I strengthened my skills for to communicate science to the world. In my free time there, I immersed myself in the specimen reference collection, examining and measuring specimens that would ultimately become an essential part of my thesis.

For my mandatory internship, I worked at an ecotourism hotel on Colombia's Pacific coast. I guided whale-watching activities and forest interpretation walks, while also conducting independent fieldwork on amphibians and reptiles in one of the most biodiverse regions on the planet. This work served as the basis f or subsequently producing a couple of publications.

Throughout my undergraduate years, I was fortunate to be mentored by Professor Juan Camilo Arredondo, a PhD graduate from the Universidade de São Paulo (USP). He consistently encouraged me to do research and publish, highlighting the strength of a well-rounded resume. Now I'm in search of a postgraduate in which I aspire to deepen my expertise in taxonomy, bioacoustics, and systematics, and to contribute to the conservation of Colombia's extraordinary biodiversity.

Influences & Affinities

A scientist is, first and foremost, a human being. Science is often presented as neutral, but the people who do it never are. Scientists are cultural and political subjects whether we admit it or not. I am shaped by the world around me like anyone else, and science is never practiced in a vacuum. We carry our histories, values, and convictions into the lab and the classroom. Rather than hide that, I prefer to be open about it. Below are the teachers, films, books, and m usic that have shaped how I think about culture, politics, and society. Some changed my mind; others simply put words to what I already felt. I honestly can't always tell which is which, and that is part of the point.

Teachers

Long before I knew what kind of scientist I wanted to be, I met people who showed me what a scientist could be. Some taught me methods and techniques. Others taught me, without meaning to, how to treat students, how to disagree, and how to care about what happens outside the lab. I don't think I would think the way I do without them. Whatever I do well, part of it started in their classrooms. Whatever I get wrong is mine.

Juan Camilo Arredondo
Vertebrate zoology
I met Juan Camilo when I took the vertebrate zoology course. To be honest, I was an average—almost mediocre—student. I hadn't done well in the prerequisite course, invertebrate zoology; I barely passed it. I must also confess that I didn't feel an instant connection with the subject matter or the professor. It took time, and I don't know exactly when it happened, but at some point a bond formed that would become one of the most important friendships of my life. He made zoology feel like something worth wanting. He talks about the people who came before him, and the ones he worked next to, with the pasion of the first moment. He is as curious about where an idea came from, its detours and forgotten corners, as about the idea itself. For him, doing things well is not about compliance. It is a way of respecting the work and everyone who built it, and I hold my own work to a standard I first saw in him. He notices what the people around him need before they say it, and he acts without turning it into a moment. He never made me feel I owed him anything. He has never asked me for anything in return and maybe that is why I take it so seriously. There is a pact between us that no one ever spoke out loud, and I intend to keep it: to work well, to be honest, and to become someone worth the time he put into me. I've come to think that is the rarest kind of friendship. Being seen like that, in times when I couldn't have asked, changed what I believed I could expect from other people, and from myself. There is a story about Carl Sagan and a young Neil deGrasse Tyson: a scientist who could have kept his distance and chose to reach back instead. I will never forget the moment he saw me with an old, torn bag and gave me the backpack he was carrying that day—my first Quechua bag, brought from Brazil. Only later I understood the lesson that he taught me that day: he showed me the kind of approachable, helpful teacher I wanna be. He is also the kind of person who tells the truth directly, without cushioning, and without ever making you feel smaller. It's the honesty of someone who respects you enough to expect more, which is why it lands as care and not as criticism. But at the same time he taught me—by example—something about adulthood that I hadn't known before meeting him: how to apologize and own up to one's errors. Words fail me when trying to list everything I have learned from him and how much our frienships mean to me.

Books

I wish I could say I’m an avid reader, but sadly, I’m slow; I lose my place and have to go back; I struggle even to start books—and sometimes to finish them. It’s true there have been books I devoured at an incredible pace, but that isn't the norm for me. Nevertheless, reading is fundamental to my life. I firmly believe it is essential for humanity, too—if only we, as a society, read more. I try to choose what I read carefully, because if you don't read much, you should at least make sure the little you do read is worthwhile.

Here you will find science books, but also literature, because ultimately, everything is connected.

Carl Sagan
I first discovered Carl Sagan when I started a degree in Engineering Physics –in reality, I had already seen him in school—coincidentally, during a math class but I wasn't ready for it back then. During my first semester of university, I was very bad at math —still am—, and to be honest, I wasn't doing well in my degree program. But while in it I started to get consumed by a sense of snobbery. I loved the way my classmates talked about science and all the wild and crazy interesting facts they knew. A recurring theme was how everyone began to love science —and physics in particular— and Carl was always a fundamental part of that. With Carl, I opened the door to a world from which there is no turning back: the world of critical and skeptical thinking. Carl also taught me to love science, to embrace it as a way of life. I still get chills and a powerful feeling washes over me when I listen to Cosmos soundtrack or rewatch an episode for the umpteenth time.

La Vorágine
José Eustasio Rivera
"La Vorágine"—what a name! To me, the word vorágine—maelstrom—is powerful in itself; long before I even knew its meaning, it stirred up whirlpools within me. And perhaps that is what I like about this book: even before reading it, I could sense the gale-force reality it portrays. This book made me want to get to know the Llanos and their culture. I already enjoyed joropo—a genre that stirs up a whirlwind of emotion within me—but I will always harbor a longing to live another life as a llanero.
Cien Años de Soledad
Gabriel García Márquez
Macondo—that name we use to refer to Colombia when the country feels unreal to us, something that happens often these days. Márquez gave us more than just a book; he gave us ways to understand and heal our wounds. He lifted us out of our ordinary state as Colombians and placed us as guards in a panopticon. From that vantage point, we recognize the people around us as his characters. This allows us to grasp, to some extent, the complexity of our history and our current reality. For me, the book’s ending is revelatory. It is bleak—an absolute fatality, marking the impossibility of a fresh start for Colombians. Like Aureliano Babilonia, Colombia seems trapped in a constant struggle to decipher the parchments of its own history, even as the winds of violence, oblivion, and repetition blow fiercely. Perhaps, once we finally understand our history, those cycles of violence will come to an end, and a new beginning will await Colombia.
El Extranjero
Albert Camus
This book came into my life at a very special moment. It happened after I had been struggling emotionally and following the persistent urging of Esteban—a great friend I made during my biology studies. Seeing the state I was in, Esteban recommended Camus to me on several occasions; however, I have always been a poor reader—I get distracted easily and fail to retain important details—so my response was always that it wasn't the kind of reading for me, or that I wouldn't understand any of it. For some reason—and it wasn't intentional—when I finally read El Extranjero, I understood everything. Meursault’s behavior was so revelatory to me that there wasn't a single line that didn't resonate with me. This book set in motion an endless process of emotional healing for me.
Films

For me, movies have been a form of education—not formal instruction per se, but an introduction to ideas or historical moments I was previously unaware of, which I later went on to explore further—as well as a source of emotional healing that transcends mere entertainment. I fervently believe that well-chosen films can be very powerful in developing empathy and serving as a cognitive and creative stimulus.

For me, this film is one of the best portrayals of the passion for science. How often—just like Dr. Arroway—have I spent hours on end in front of computers or stereoscopes, never wanting to stop? Furthermore, it offers a firsthand look at some of society's greatest dilemmas: the existence of other life forms in the universe and the tension between science and faith.

Music

Music, of course, cannot be left out. And although there is no such thing as "scientific music" per se, these two spheres have been connected for millennia and complement each other in various ways. For any scientist, music is an integral part of the work; we would go (even more) mad without it. Personally, I love listening to Cumbias, Boleros, Porros, Currulaos, Vallenatos, Joropo and Salsa—among many other genres—whether I am out in the field working with nature or in the laboratory, marveling at what I am learning and discovering.This list could easily be as long as my playlist, but here I’ve selected some of the songs and artists that move me the most.

Yo me llamo cumbia
Totó la Momposina
To me, this song represents that feeling of wanting to be one with the world around us—which, in a way, we are. Beyond that, it is exquisite; it is clever in its play on the similarities between a woman and a Cumbia. To me, women are, in a way, a Cumbia.
El Mochuelo
Otto Serge and Rafael Ricardo
Vallenato has been part of my life since I was little—back when my aunts would listen to it non-stop on weekends. Yet, at some point, Colombians went through a phase where we felt ashamed of this music; to me, it stems from regionalism—a kind of chauvinism that makes us embarrassed by what is truly our own, by our roots and our native culture. Rediscovering vallenato has been a deeply moving and beautiful journey for me. This particular song conjures up places I have never actually seen, yet somehow revisit in my mind—as if a natural landscape could be reconstructed entirely through the music.
La Noche De Los Cocuyos
Juan Harvey Caicedo
Even though the Colombian Llanos constitute a vast region with immense cultural and environmental diversity, most of us Colombians from the center of the country are largely unfamiliar with its culture. I was fortunate enough to have an uncle who loved the poems of Juan Harvey Caicedo, which kept this region constantly on my mind. More recently, having studied biology, my fascination—and perhaps even a touch of snobbery—regarding this and the country's other regions has only grown. And really, how could it not, when you hear this poem narrated by such an iconic voice?

Research

Research
Focal areas

The acoustic environment of tropical forests is among the richest and most biologically complex on Earth. A significant portion of this soundscape is produced by anurans — the group that includes all frogs and toads. The most common type of vocalization in this group is the advertisement call, a signal males produce to attract mates and deter rivals. Because females of a given species preferentially respond to the calls of conspecific males, advertisement calls act as prezygotic barriers — mechanisms that prevent reproduction between individuals of different species before fertilization can occur, and therefore play a central role in the origin and maintenance of species boundaries. The central focus of my research so far is to understand how these vocalizations can be leveraged to address fundamental questions in taxonomy, systematics, and natural history.

Fieldwork
Across Colombia
Bioacoustics as a tool for taxonomy and systematics
Recording frog calls in Huina, Bahía Solano, Chocó, Colombia.

Advertisement calls are species-specific signals shaped by sexual selection and reproductive isolation, making them powerful to delimit species boundaries, uncover cryptic diversity, and infer evolutionary relationships. A critical first step in applying bioacoustics to systematics is the formal description of calls for species whose vocalizations remain unknown. Much of my work is dedicated to filling these gaps — documenting the advertisement calls of poorly known Colombian anurans and establishing the acoustic baselines necessary to use sound as a diagnostic character.

A work done with colleagues on the granular glassfrog Cochranella granulosa revealed geographic call variation across its distribution in Colombia, suggesting hidden diversity and highlighting the importance of integrating acoustic data into taxonomic revisions. I have also described advertisement calls for understudied species such as Serranobatrachus sanctaemartae and distress calls for Smilisca phaeota.

Taxonomy, Biological Collections, and the Importance of Historical Research
Work in collections revisiting material.

Sound characters alone cannot resolve taxonomic problems if the foundational question of which species we are talking about remains unanswered. A persistent challenge in tropical biodiversity science is the existence of multiple overlapping shortfalls: the Linnean shortfall — the vast number of species still awaiting formal description — and the Wallacean shortfall — our incomplete knowledge of where known species actually occur.

I am part of a new generation of researchers committed to practicing taxonomy as a rigorous, integrative science. Central to this vision is the role of biological collections — not only as repositories of historical specimens, but as active scientific infrastructure. Enriching collections in developing countries is not merely a curatorial concern: it is a prerequisite for rigorous, locally grounded taxonomy.

Science Communication and Public Engagement
Science outreach with school students.

"Science is a way of thinking much more than it is a body of knowledge." — Carl Sagan

This is, perhaps, the most underappreciated truth in science communication. What science actually has to offer the public is not a collection of data; it is a way of approaching the world — with curiosity, with skepticism, with a willingness to revise what you think you know in the face of new evidence.

In the Chocó, working as a naturalist guide during humpback whale season, I rarely led with facts. Instead, I tried to lead with consequences and questions. At the Museo de Ciencias Naturales de La Salle in Medellín, I worked with adults and children who often arrived not knowing what a natural history collection is or why it exists. Helping visitors understand the connection between a pinned frog in a drawer and a policy that protects a watershed is, I think, one of the most political acts a scientist can perform.
